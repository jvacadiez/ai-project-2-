01 The basics

What is artificial intelligence?

Artificial intelligence, or AI, describes computer systems designed to perform tasks that normally require human abilities, such as recognising patterns, understanding language, making predictions or solving problems.

Many AI systems learn from large collections of examples. During training, they identify statistical patterns in text, images or other data, then use those patterns to produce an answer or prediction. The OECD’s explanation of AI (opens in a new tab) provides more detail.

AI does not understand the world like a person. A chatbot generates language by predicting suitable sequences of words. It may sound confident even when its information is incomplete or incorrect. AI output is a useful suggestion, not automatic proof.

02 The opportunities

How can AI help?

AI can process information quickly and assist people with repetitive or time-consuming work.

A

Health & access

In healthcare, it can help specialists examine medical images and notice important patterns. Accessibility tools can create captions, read text aloud or describe images. Translation systems can help people communicate across languages.

B

Work & study

At work, AI can summarise documents, organise notes and help create a first draft for a person to improve. When studying, I could use it to simplify a difficult concept, generate practice questions or compare arguments.

Asking AI to complete an assignment without understanding or verifying it would weaken learning.

AI can also support research and energy management. Its greatest benefit comes from combining computer speed with human knowledge and judgement.

03 The concerns

What are the challenges and risks?

Errors & bias

AI can produce convincing errors, sometimes called “hallucinations,” and repeat biases in its training data. Biased systems used in recruitment, lending or public services can unfairly affect people’s opportunities.

Privacy

Users may accidentally share personal, confidential or copyrighted information. Organisations must consider how information is collected, stored and reused.

Security

AI can help attackers write deceptive messages or search for software weaknesses, while defenders can use it to detect suspicious activity. The result depends on its design, access and supervision.

Case study / 2024

When seeing is no longer believing

Generative AI can create realistic fake voices, photographs and videos. In 2024, Hong Kong police reported that scammers impersonated company leaders in a fake video meeting. An employee transferred approximately HK$200 million.

A familiar face on screen is no longer sufficient verification. Important financial requests should be confirmed separately and require more than one person’s approval.

Read the government report ↗ (opens in a new tab)

The US National Institute of Standards and Technology says trustworthy AI should be reliable, safe, secure, transparent, privacy-enhancing and fair. Its AI Risk Management Framework (opens in a new tab) helps organisations evaluate these issues.

04 Looking ahead

What might the future look like?

Verified fact

AI requires data centres with powerful computers. The International Energy Agency estimates that their global electricity use could rise from about 485 terawatt-hours in 2025 to 950 in 2030, around 3% of global demand. Read the IEA’s analysis (opens in a new tab).

My interpretation

AI will not automatically cause blackouts. However, rapid local demand could pressure grids if data centres grow faster than power generation, storage and transmission. Companies and governments should plan their locations, improve efficiency and publish their energy and water use.

Prediction

AI will probably become a normal assistant in education, healthcare and office work. People will need to check sources, protect private information, recognise manipulation and know when not to automate a decision.

Dependence on a few cloud and AI providers may make outages more disruptive. Critical systems need backups, clear limits and human control. The best future expands what people can do without replacing human responsibility.

05 Behind the page

How this website was made

I organised the site as a focused explainer: it begins with a definition, moves through benefits and risks, and finishes with the future. I separated facts, interpretation and prediction so readers can distinguish evidence from opinion.

The visual design uses short sections, clear headings, comfortable spacing and callout boxes. A limited colour palette and strong contrast improve readability. Semantic HTML elements such as header, main, section and footer give the page structure. CSS creates the responsive layout, typography and colours for phones and larger screens.

After testing, I shortened paragraphs, improved the heading order, checked descriptive links, adjusted mobile spacing and reviewed colour contrast.

AI assisted with brainstorming, structure and editing, but I did not treat its output as automatically correct. I compared claims with OECD, NIST, IEA and Hong Kong government sources, rewrote the explanations and kept human responsibility central.
